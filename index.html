IDAF Chemie & Geschichte

Interdisciplinary Project

This project explores chemistry and history together through the development of an energy cell experiment and its link to the evolution of electric vehicles.

About Our Group and Goal

We are a group consisting of Fabian, Nicky, Levin, and Marin.

For our IDAF (Interdisziplinarische Allgemeine Facharbeit) with the theme chemistry and history, we first investigated the chemical reaction in an energy cell we created using steel wool, dense saltwater, and nails while passing energy through it.

This led us to the topic of historical electric cars and what has changed over time culturally, historically, and chemically.

BMW i7 Powered by Hydrogen

The hydrogen-powered BMW i7 idea would use a fuel cell to turn hydrogen from high-pressure tanks into electricity for an electric motor, with a small battery covering peak power demand; the only direct emission is water vapor. It failed to become a successful series product because hydrogen refueling stations are still rare, fuel-cell and tank systems remain expensive, and the full energy chain from electricity to hydrogen and back to electricity is less efficient than charging a battery-electric car directly.

Major Turning Points in Electric & Hydrogen Car History

  • 1830s: First experimental electric vehicles appear in Europe.
  • 1890s: Electric city cars and taxis become a serious transport option.
  • 1908–1920s: Cheap gasoline and mass production push electric cars out of the market.
  • 1970s: Oil crises and pollution concerns restart electric and hydrogen research.
  • 2008: Modern battery-electric performance cars prove long-range EVs can compete.
  • 2014 onward: Fuel-cell cars enter real markets, but infrastructure remains limited.

1835 — Early Electric Prototypes

Inventors in the 1830s built some of the first battery-powered road vehicles, proving that electric movement was technically possible. These machines were slow and heavy, and their batteries could not be recharged in practical ways. Even so, this period introduced the core idea of clean, quiet propulsion without direct combustion.

1897 — Electric Taxis in Urban Transport

At the end of the 19th century, electric taxis operated in cities like New York and London. Drivers liked them because they were easier to start and cleaner than early gasoline cars. This was the first time electric vehicles scaled as a visible public transport theme rather than a laboratory experiment.

1908 — Model T Era and the Decline of EVs

Ford's mass-produced Model T dramatically lowered gasoline car prices and changed mobility economics. At the same time, cheap oil and expanding fuel networks made long-distance travel much easier for combustion engines. Electric cars could not keep up in range, speed, or cost, so they largely disappeared from mainstream use.

1973 — Oil Crisis and New Energy Thinking

The oil shocks of the 1970s exposed how fragile dependence on petroleum could be. Governments, universities, and car companies restarted research programs for electric drivetrains and hydrogen fuel concepts. This turning point connected transport directly to energy security and environmental policy debates.

2008 — Lithium-Ion Breakthrough in Road Cars

Modern lithium-ion battery systems enabled electric cars to deliver both practical range and strong performance. The Tesla Roadster became a global symbol that EVs could be desirable, not just experimental. This shifted public perception and forced large manufacturers to invest seriously in full battery-electric models.

2014 — Fuel-Cell Cars Reach Customers

Vehicles such as the Toyota Mirai showed that hydrogen fuel-cell cars could be sold to real customers with quick refueling and water-only tailpipe emissions. The technology proved functional, especially for users needing fast refills. However, high vehicle costs and sparse hydrogen stations still limit widespread adoption.
